At some point as a child I went camping with my dad - this wasn't unusual as we often spent our time camping or hiking. Growing up in Middle-England, my dad used to refer to the Peak District as his back garden. For this particular camping holiday we went to Bala in North Wales. While there we did the usual holiday stuff - hiking and taking in the scenery. On one such hike - and for the life of me I've got no idea which mountain it was that we walked up - we must have stumbled upon the [Mach loop](https://www.machloop.co.uk/). For anyone who isn't aware, the Mach loop is a low level flight training area for the Royal Air Force. I have a very distinct memory of coming over the crest of a hill while out on this hike, only to be greeted by a fighter jet flying at pretty much eye level. I was probably about 11 at the time and I became completely enamoured with aircraft. The idea that *people* made that and that *people* flew in them was the most amazing thing to me.

Fast forward a few years and I'm starting to think about my future and I decide I want to join the RAF. They have a fast track programme for pilots that requires you to have some form of stem degree so I applied to universities. Originally, I was going to physics as that was my best subject at school. However, while visiting one of the universities, I found that they had courses in aeronautical and aerospace engineering and I thought "why do general physics when I could do some aircraft related?". That's how I ended up at the University of Leeds studying aerospace engineering.

I enjoy my job - it's ultimately professional problem solving. However, I sometimes feel that I speak a slightly different language to most of the health economics world. I put that down to my meandering technical background, which I think gives me a fairly unique perspective on modelling.















I've now spent roughly equal halves of my working life in two fields that, on the surface, have almost nothing in common. In the first, I was an engineer: I built simulations of fluids, structures, and — for reasons that still surprise me — fish. In the second, I build health economic models that help decide whether a treatment is worth funding. Aerospace engineering and health economics don't share conferences, vocabulary, or dress code.

What they do share is the thing I actually do all day: build a model of something too complex to reason about by hand, and then trust it enough to make a decision with real consequences. And having crossed from one world to the other, I've been struck by how much each field could learn from the other's habits.

This post is about the direction people expect least. When I tell engineers I moved into health economics, there's sometimes a faint assumption that I traded "hard" modelling for something softer. That assumption is wrong, and this post is my argument for why. Here are three things health economics does genuinely better than the engineering I came from — habits I wish I could ship back across the divide.
